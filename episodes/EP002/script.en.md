## S02 — Roadmap | 0:14–0:52 | 94 words

Those letters were Hangul, the Korean alphabet, and the royal court itself had introduced them roughly sixty years earlier. [F1][F6] Today, Hangul is celebrated with a national holiday. [F14] But between its invention and that holiday lie more than four centuries in which the alphabet was mocked by scholars, banned by a king, pushed to the margins, and finally renamed. [F5][F6][F7][F9] This year marks 580 years since Hangul was promulgated, and 100 years since the first Hangul Day. [F16] So let's walk through those 580 years, and at the end, I'll show you the simplest way to try the alphabet yourself.

- 화면: 가로 타임라인 1446 → 1504 → 1894 → 1926 → 2026. 점이 하나씩 켜지는 모션. 배경에 흐린 자모 패턴.
- 텍스트: "1446 · 1504 · 1894 · 1926 · 2026" / "580 years · 100th Hangul Day".
- 장면 메모: "roughly sixty years"는 1446→1504 계산값. 상품은 여기서 예고만("try the alphabet yourself"), 상품명 없음.

## S03 — Why a king invents an alphabet | 0:52–2:04 | 180 words

Rewind to the 1440s. Korea was the kingdom of Joseon, and writing meant Chinese characters. In 1443, King Sejong presented his court with something new: a set of twenty-eight letters designed for the Korean language. [F1][F2] Three years later, in 1446, it was promulgated in a book called Hunminjeongeum, "The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People." [F1]

The preface, attributed to Sejong, explains why. According to it, ordinary people had things to say but could not get them written down, so he made twenty-eight letters that anyone could learn easily and use every day. [F3] The commentary that accompanied it, the Haerye, puts the learning curve even more bluntly: a clever person can pick the letters up in a morning, and even a slow learner needs only ten days. [F4]

If you've watched the drama Tree with Deep Roots, from 2011, or the film The King's Letters, from 2019, you've seen dramatized versions of this project: the scholars, the arguments, the king's motives. [F19][F20] The film's take, that a Buddhist monk helped create the alphabet, is a cinematic interpretation, not settled history. [F20]

- 화면: 15세기 서재 일러스트, 책상 위 종이와 붓. 왕은 뒷모습 실루엣(얼굴·표준영정·동상 금지). 책 표지 모양 카드에 "訓民正音" 대신 한글 "훈민정음" 텍스트 오버레이. 드라마·영화는 텍스트 카드만(스틸·포스터·배우 이미지 금지).
- 텍스트: "1443 · 28 letters" → "1446 · Hunminjeongeum" → 인용 카드 "a morning … ten days — Haerye" → "Tree with Deep Roots (SBS, 2011) · The King's Letters (2019)".
- 장면 메모: 인용은 "the Haerye says" 톤 유지(화자 특정 금지, F4 △). 서문 인용(F3)은 단일 출처라 "attributed / according to"를 자막에도 유지. 배우 이름 금지.
- TTS: Joseon = "jo-sun", Sejong = "seh-jong", Hunminjeongeum = "hoon-min-jung-eum", Haerye = "heh-ryeh".

## S04 — Twenty-eight letters, twenty-four today | 2:04–2:40 | 91 words

Let's look at what he actually made. Twenty-eight letters: seventeen consonants and eleven vowels. [F2] Here they are on screen. The ones in white are still in use today. The four in grey are not: over the centuries they fell out of use, and modern Korean gets by with twenty-four. [F2] Keep those four in mind. They'll come back at the end.

What matters for our story is that the alphabet was built to be learned quickly, by people who were not scholars. [F3][F4] And that, as it turned out, was exactly the problem.

- 화면: 검은 배경에 28자 자모가 격자로 등장(획 순서대로 그려지는 모션). 현행 24자 흰색, 폐지 4자 "ㆁ ㅿ ㆆ ㆍ" 회색으로 뒤늦게 켜졌다 흐려짐. 글자는 프로그램 텍스트 오버레이(AI 생성 이미지에 글자 넣지 않음).
- 텍스트: "17 consonants + 11 vowels = 28" → "today: 24" → 회색 4자 옆 "no longer used".
- 장면 메모: 자모 글꼴은 상용 라이선스 확인. 폐지 4자는 유니코드 ㆁ(U+3181) ㅿ(U+317F) ㆆ(U+3186) ㆍ(U+318D 아래아)로 표시 가능한 글꼴 필요. 이 장면이 숏폼 "사라진 네 글자" 컷의 원본.

## S05 — 1444: "Only barbarians have their own script" | 2:40–3:40 | 151 words

The first objection came within months. In 1444, a group of senior scholars led by Choe Malli, a deputy director of the Hall of Worthies, the royal research institute, submitted a petition against the new letters. [F5] Their argument tells you a lot about how the elite saw the world. Having a script of your own, they wrote, was something only barbarians did. The Mongols, the Jurchens, the Japanese, the Tibetans: those peoples had their own writing. In their logic, civilized people wrote in Chinese characters. [F5]

Notice the logic, because it's the heart of the whole conflict. To Choe Malli and his colleagues, an easy alphabet was not progress. It was a step away from the civilization they belonged to. Sejong pressed ahead anyway, and the alphabet was promulgated two years later. [F1][F5] But the attitude in that petition did not go away. It would shape how the alphabet was treated for the next four hundred years. [F7][F8]

- 화면: 두루마리 상소문 일러스트(글자는 읽히지 않는 흐림). 지도 위에 몽골·여진·일본·티베트 영역이 차례로 표시되고 각각의 문자 모양(일반화한 획) 아이콘. 인물 얼굴 없음.
- 텍스트: "1444 · petition" → "Mongols · Jurchens · Japanese · Tibetans" → "'only barbarians'". 민족명 나열은 상소문의 표현 인용임을 자막에 따옴표로 표시.
- 장면 메모: 상소문 논리를 사료 인용으로만 다룬다. 현대 한일·한중 비교로 확장 금지. "four hundred years"는 1446→1894 범위. 최만리 사망일(단일 출처) 미사용.
- TTS: Choe Malli = "chweh mal-lee".

## S06 — 1504: The ban | 3:40–4:42 | 155 words

Now back to 1504. The king was Yeonsangun, and on the nineteenth of July, three anonymous notices criticizing him were discovered. [F6] They were written in the alphabet. [F6]

Here's my read on why that mattered so much from the throne's point of view. Criticism in Chinese characters could only come from the educated few, and those people could be identified. Criticism in an alphabet anyone could learn in days could come from anywhere, from anyone. [F4][F6] Yeonsangun's response was to ban the alphabet outright: no learning it, no teaching it, no using it. [F6]

The ban did not last. By December of the same year, the same king ordered the calendar translated into the alphabet, which in practice meant the ban was over after about five months. [F6] But it marked something. The script a king had created to give his people a voice had now been treated by another king as a threat, precisely because it worked. [F3][F6]

- 화면: S01의 돌담·벽서 3장으로 복귀 → 종이가 찢겨 떨어지는 모션 → "학습·교육·사용" 3단어가 차례로 지워짐 → 달력(역서) 페이지 위에 한글 자모가 다시 나타남. 왕은 실루엣, 얼굴 없음.
- 텍스트: "July 19, 1504" → "no learning · no teaching · no using" → "December 1504 · calendar in Hangul" → "about 5 months".
- 장면 메모: 처벌 조항(참수 등)은 단일 출처라 낭독·자막 모두 넣지 않는다. 연산군 인물 평가(폭군 등) 서술 금지, 사료 기록만. "Here's my read" 단락은 해석 표시.
- TTS: Yeonsangun = "yun-san-goon".

## S07 — Surviving as "vernacular writing" | 4:42–5:26 | 110 words

So what happened to the alphabet between 1504 and the modern era? Officially, not much. The court and the bureaucracy mostly went back to Chinese characters, and the alphabet picked up a nickname: eonmun, "vernacular writing," which, to my ear, ranked it below the real thing. [F7]

But it did not disappear. According to the historical accounts, its early users were mostly the people the scholars' world had left out: women, and Buddhist communities. [F7] That's the pattern you'll see for the next few centuries: the alphabet survived not at the top of society, but underneath it. No one at court was promoting it. People simply used it, because it was easy. [F7]

- 화면: 화면 상단은 한자가 빽빽한 공문서 일러스트(실제 문장 아님), 하단은 한글 편지와 불경 두루마리 일러스트. 두 층이 분리된 구도. 인물은 실루엣.
- 텍스트: "언문 · eonmun · 'vernacular writing'" / "court: Chinese characters ↑ / everyday life: Hangul ↓".
- 장면 메모: F7은 학술 출처 1곳이라 "According to the historical accounts", "mostly" 유지. 소설·문학 사용 확산은 F7 범위 밖이라 언급하지 않음. 문해율 숫자 절대 금지(research §3 집계행).
- TTS: eonmun = "un-moon".

## S08 — 1894: "National script" | 5:26–5:58 | 80 words

The turn came in November 1894, during a period of sweeping government reforms. A royal edict declared that official documents would be written in the national script first, with a mixture of Korean letters and Chinese characters allowed alongside. [F8] After four hundred and forty-eight years, the alphabet that had been promulgated in 1446 was, for the first time, the state's official script. [F1][F8]

Notice the wording: "national script," gungmun. Not "vernacular writing." The name itself had been promoted. [F7][F8]

- 화면: 공문서 일러스트 위에 한글이 먼저, 한자가 작게 뒤따르는 레이아웃 전환 모션. S07의 "언문" 텍스트가 "국문"으로 바뀌는 타이포 모션.
- 텍스트: "November 1894" → "국문 · gungmun · 'national script'" → "1446 → 1894: 448 years".
- 장면 메모: 칙령 일자 "11월 21일"은 단일 출처라 월까지만 낭독·표기. 갑오개혁의 정치 배경(외세·전쟁)은 다루지 않는다. "448 years"는 계산값.
- TTS: gungmun = "goong-moon".

## S09 — A new name and a first holiday | 5:58–6:34 | 89 words

The name we use today came a little later. Around 1912, the linguist Ju Si-gyeong coined the word Hangul. [F9] Han means "great," and geul means "letters" or "writing." [F9] Great letters. From a nickname that meant "vernacular" to a name that meant "great" in roughly twenty years. [F7][F8][F9]

Then, in 1926, a society of Korean language scholars, not a government, created a day to celebrate the alphabet. They called it Gagya Day. [F10] It was later renamed Hangul Day. [F10] That 1926 date is why 2026 is the hundredth anniversary of Hangul Day. [F16]

## S10 — The lost manual and the date on the calendar | 6:34–7:32 | 145 words

Here's the twist I like most in this story. The Haerye, the commentary I quoted earlier, is the book that explains how the letters were designed, and for centuries no copy of it was known to survive. [F12]
