## S03 — Scene one: One Fine Spring Day | 0:45–1:49 | 160 words

Scene one: One Fine Spring Day, a 2001 film directed by Hur Jin-ho. [F7] Eun-su, played by Lee Young-ae, asks Sang-woo, played by Yoo Ji-tae: "Ramyeon meogeullaeyo?" Do you want to eat some ramyeon? [F7]

On the surface, it's an offer of food. But over the years, the line picked up a second meaning. In Korea, it became a playful way of inviting someone to stay a little longer. [F7]

Here's what I find interesting. The line only works because ramyeon is so ordinary. Nobody needs an excuse to make a pot of instant noodles. That makes it the perfect cover for a question that's really about something else. You can say it out loud and still leave the other person room to answer, "Sure, I'm a bit hungry."

## S04 — The shortcut theory | 1:49–2:48 | 149 words

So why do Korean stories reach for ramyeon so often? Here's my theory, and it starts with a number.

According to the World Instant Noodles Association, as reported by the Korea Times, South Koreans ate 79.2 servings of instant noodles per person in 2024. That's the second-highest figure in the world, just behind Vietnam at 81. [F14] Do the math, and it works out to about one and a half servings a week for every person in the country. [F14]

When something is that familiar, it turns into shorthand. A character opening a packet of ramyeon tells a Korean audience a lot without a single line of dialogue: it's late, it's casual, this is everyday life. Nobody watching needs ramyeon explained. You just put the pot on the stove.

And because everyone knows what ramyeon usually means, a story can twist that meaning. That's exactly what the line from 2001 does.

- 화면: 막대 2개 "Vietnam 81 / South Korea 79.2 (2024)" → 달력 1주 칸에 그릇 아이콘 1.5개.
- 텍스트: 출처 표기 "WINA, via Korea Times". "1.5 a week" 옆에 "(79.2 ÷ 52)" 작게.

## S05 — Scene two: Parasite | 2:48–3:53 | 161 words

Scene two: Parasite, from 2019. The film features a dish called jjapaguri, a combination of two Nongshim instant noodles, Chapagetti and Neoguri. [F9]

Now picture the job of whoever is writing the English subtitles. You have a Korean nickname built out of two brand names, and an audience around the world, many of whom may never have seen either packet. What do you write?

Translator Darcy Paquet's answer was to make up a word. In the English subtitles, jjapaguri became "ram-don": ramyeon plus udon. [F9]

I love this detail. To me, it shows how specific a ramyeon scene can be: the dish needed a brand-new English word just to travel.

And travel it did. On February 10 and 11, 2020, right after the Academy Awards, sales of Neoguri and Chapagetti packets at the convenience store chain GS25 rose about 60 percent from the year before, as reported by Asia Economy. [F10] People didn't just watch the scene. They went out and bought the noodles.

- 화면: 무지(無地) 봉지 2개가 합쳐져 한 그릇이 되는 모션(상표·패키지 디자인 금지). 자막 카드 위에서 "jjapaguri"가 "ram-don"으로 바뀌는 타이핑 효과.
- 텍스트: "ramyeon + udon = ram-don", "GS25 · Feb 10–11, 2020 · about +60% YoY (Asia Economy)". 로고 대신 글자로만 표기.

## S10 — Shin Ramyun at 40 | 6:48–7:53 | 163 words

Which brings us to this month. Nongshim launched Shin Ramyun in October 1986, so October 2026 marks its 40th anniversary. [F1] Its developers started from sogogi jangguk, a Korean beef soup, when they designed the broth. [F2] It took first place in Korea's ramyeon market in 1991 and held it for 35 years straight, through 2025. [F3] By the end of 2025, it had sold about 42.5 billion packs. [F4] It's sold in more than 100 countries, and about 40 percent of its roughly 20 trillion won in cumulative sales has come from outside Korea. [F5]

For the anniversary, Nongshim released Shin Ramyun Rosé, an idea that came from customers' own recipes. It went on sale in Korea and Japan first, in May 2026. [F6] And in late August, at Gamescom 2026 in Cologne, the company set up a hands-on "Shin Ramyun factory" booth. [F20]

Notice a pattern? Jjapaguri is two packets combined. [F9] Rosé started as a customer recipe. [F6] Ramyeon is something people keep remixing, on screen and at home.
