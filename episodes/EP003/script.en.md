Picture late November in Korea. Tarps across a courtyard, plastic tubs, a wall of cabbages. By nightfall, they'll be kimchi for the whole winter. [F2] And the date wasn't set by a calendar. It was set by the weather. [F5]

## S02 — What kimjang is · series open | 0:15–1:07 | 130 words

This is kimjang: the stretch of late autumn when Korean households and neighbors make kimchi, salted and seasoned vegetables, in large batches, together, and share it out. [F2][F3] In 2013, UNESCO added it to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity. [F1] If you've only ever seen kimchi on a plate, kimjang is the other end of it: the making.

Welcome to the first episode of Korea's Calendar, a series about the dates Korea arranges its year around. Kimjang is the right place to start, because it isn't really one day. It's a year of preparation that comes together in late autumn. [F3] Today: how the weather picks the date, why kimchi wasn't always red, what UNESCO actually listed, and what it means that fewer households plan to make it themselves. [F18]

- 사실: F1 ✅, F2 ✅, F3 ✅(소금·젓갈·고추 → "salted and seasoned"), F18 ✅(로드맵 예고만, 수치는 S08).

## S03 — The weather sets the date | 1:07–2:10 | 156 words

Start with the date, because there isn't a fixed one. Korean weather forecasters use a rule of thumb: the time for kimjang is when the daily average temperature stays at four degrees Celsius or below, and the daily low falls to zero or below. [F5] That's roughly 39 and 32 degrees Fahrenheit. So the signal isn't a day on the calendar. It's the cold settling in. Think of it as waiting for the first stretch that actually feels like winter.

That's why there's such a thing as a kimjang forecast. Reportedly, the national weather service used to issue it, and the job has since passed to private forecasters. [F7] And according to forecasters' records, the right time in Seoul has drifted later over the decades. [F6] Hold on to that; it comes back.

Here's my read: this is what turns kimjang into a calendar event. The weather opens a window, and everything has to be ready when it does.

- 사실: F5 ✅(4℃·0℃, 화씨는 단순 환산). F7 단일 → "Reportedly", 연도(2016) 미사용. F6 △ → "according to forecasters' records", 일수(4일/12일) 미사용.
- 이미지: 한지 위 온도계 두 개(평균·최저). 눈금이 기준선에 닿으면 민화식 바람·첫서리 모티프. 이어서 가로 시간축의 '적기' 점이 오른쪽(늦은 쪽)으로 조금 밀리는 모션. 눈금 간격에 일수를 표시하지 않는다.
- 화면 텍스트: "Daily average ≤ 4°C (39°F)" / "Daily low ≤ 0°C (32°F)" / 출처 "Source: Weatheri (2019); Sidae Ilbo (2014, citing KMA)" / "Kimjang forecast: national weather service → private forecasters (reportedly)" / "Seoul: later than it used to be".
- 메모: 기상업체 로고 금지(출처 캡션엔 이름 텍스트만). 마지막 문단은 해석.

## S04 — A year in the making | 2:10–3:06 | 141 words

But the cold window is only the last step. UNESCO's description of kimjang lays out a cycle that runs through the whole year. [F3] It reads less like a recipe than a planner.

In spring, it's shrimp and anchovies, salted and left to ferment into jeotgal, a salted seafood seasoning. In summer, it's sea salt. In late summer, red chili peppers are dried and ground into powder. [F3] Then, in late autumn, it all comes together, in amounts meant to carry a household through the winter. [F2]

It works like a relay. Spring hands off to summer, summer to late summer, and all of it to the first real cold. By the time the cabbages arrive, the seafood has had months to ferment. [F3]

From the outside, kimjang looks like one busy weekend. Up close, it's a schedule that runs for most of the year.

- 사실: F3 ✅(봄 새우·멸치 젓갈, 여름 천일염, 늦여름 고추 건조·분쇄), F2 ✅. "months to ferment"는 F3의 봄→늦가을 간격에서 나온 계산이다.
- 이미지: S02 원형 달력이 돌아가며 칸마다 민화 정물이 놓인다. 봄은 새우·멸치와 작은 옹기, 여름은 염전과 소금 더미, 늦여름은 멍석 위에서 마르는 붉은 고추, 늦가을은 배추. 칸 사이로 붓 획 모양 바통이 넘어가는 모션.
- 화면 텍스트: "Spring · shrimp & anchovies → jeotgal" / "Summer · sea salt" / "Late summer · chili, dried & ground" / "Late autumn · kimjang" / 출처 "Source: UNESCO, RL 00881".
- 메모: 소금·고춧가루 포대에 상표나 포장 디자인을 모사하지 않는다.

## S05 — Kimchi wasn't always red | 3:06–4:13 | 167 words

Now, about that chili powder. If you picture kimchi, you probably picture red. But for much of its recorded history, kimchi wasn't red. [F8][F9][F10]

One of the earliest written records of kimchi is a thirteenth-century poem by Yi Gyu-bo that mentions pickled radish. [F8] Chili doesn't enter the record for centuries after that: its first written mention in Korea comes in 1614, in a book called Jibong yuseol. [F9] How it reached Korea is still debated. [F9] Sources differ on when chili became a standard kimchi ingredient, but it seems to have happened only in the last two centuries or so. [F10]

Even the napa cabbage, the pale, tightly packed head most people imagine, is reportedly a late arrival. [F11] And one UNESCO description speaks of hundreds of variants of kimchi today. [F12]

So the red cabbage kimchi that stands for Korea is a fairly recent version of a much older habit: putting vegetables away for the cold months. [F2][F8] "Traditional" kimchi depends on which century you ask. The recipe kept changing. The deadline didn't.

- 사실: F8 ✅. F9 ✅("first written mention"으로만 쓰고, 전래 경로는 "still debated"만). F10 △ → "Sources differ … seems … two centuries or so". F11 단일 → "reportedly a late arrival"(19세기 말 연대는 쓰지 않음). F12 △ → UNESCO 문서 인용 "hundreds of variants"(180+ 숫자 쓰지 않음).
- 이미지: 가로로 펼친 한지 두루마리 타임라인. 13세기 칸에는 흰 무 절임 그릇과 글자를 읽을 수 없는 시 두루마리. 1614 칸에는 표지 글자 없는 책 한 권과 고추 한 송이. 그 오른쪽으로 김치 그릇 색이 흰색에서 붉은색으로 서서히 바뀌는 그라데이션(경계 연도는 표시하지 않음). 끝에 통배추 실루엣. 마지막엔 서로 다른 김치 그릇 여러 개(개수 표시 없음).
- 화면 텍스트: "13th century · Yi Gyu-bo's poem: pickled radish" / "1614 · Jibong yuseol: first written mention of chili" / "Chili in kimchi: roughly the last 200 years (sources differ)" / "Napa cabbage: a late arrival (reportedly)" / "'Hundreds of variants' (UNESCO)".

## S06 — What UNESCO actually listed | 4:13–5:20 | 167 words

Which brings us back to UNESCO. What it added to its list in December 2013, at a committee meeting in Baku, Azerbaijan, wasn't kimchi. The listing's full name is "Kimjang, making and sharing kimchi in the Republic of Korea." [F1]

UNESCO's description says late autumn is kimjang season, when communities make large quantities of kimchi together and share it, so that every household has enough for the winter. [F2] It says kimchi cuts across differences of class and region. And it notes that the know-how has mainly been passed down from mothers-in-law to daughters-in-law. [F2] In other words, a family skill, handed down in person.

Two years later, in 2015, at a meeting in Windhoek, Namibia, UNESCO inscribed a separate element: the tradition of kimchi-making in North Korea. [F4] So kimchi-making is on the list twice.

Here's the part I find most interesting. What's being recognized isn't a recipe. It's a gathering. A recipe can be written down and kept. A gathering only exists as long as people keep showing up.

- 사실: F1 ✅(2013-12, 제8차 위원회, 바쿠, 등재명). F2 ✅(늦가을·공동체·나눔·계층/지역·시어머니→며느리 전승). F4 ✅(2015 빈트후크, 별도 등재. "덜 빨갛다"는 단일이라 쓰지 않음).

## S07 — From the ground to the fridge | 5:20–6:06 | 116 words

Traditionally, kimchi went into jars buried in the ground. Below the surface, the temperature holds steady through the winter, close to zero, within about a degree either way. [F13] The earth did the refrigerating.

But you can't bury a jar from the twelfth floor. The first commercial kimchi refrigerator reportedly went on sale in 1995. [F14] The idea was to copy the ground: to use direct cooling to hold kimchi at that same steady, near-freezing temperature. [F13]

Here's the twist I like: a modern appliance was built to imitate a hole in the yard. The ground moved indoors, and kimjang moved with it. The fridge solved where to keep the kimchi. It didn't change who had to make it.

## S08 — What's changing | 6:06–7:04 | 146 words

So the cold now comes from a plug. What's changing?

Start with the numbers. According to the Korea Rural Economic Institute, the share of households planning to make their own kimjang fell from 64.5 percent in 2024 to 62.3 percent in 2025. [F18] One report this September called it a kimchi crisis. [F18] I'd put it more carefully. Six in ten households is still a clear majority, and two data points show a direction, not a collapse.

The pressure isn't only in the kitchen. According to Korean news reports, the highland summer-cabbage fields of Gangwon Province have shrunk sharply in recent years, with climate change and crop disease blamed. [F19] And the date the weather sets? In Seoul, as we saw, it has drifted later. [F6]

Notice what that survey measures. Not whether people eat kimchi, but whether they make it themselves. And that is the part UNESCO actually listed. [F1][F18]

## S09 — A second date: November 22 | 7:04–7:42 | 95 words

There's a newer date on the calendar, too. In 2020, Korea designated November 22 as Kimchi Day by law. [F16] The date has traveled. In 2021, California's state assembly passed a resolution recognizing Kimchi Day, and Virginia, Maryland, New York and Washington, D.C. have also proclaimed November 22 as Kimchi Day. [F17]

So kimjang now sits between two kinds of dates. One is fixed by law and marked from Seoul to California. The other moves every year, and the weather sets it. [F5][F16] A fixed date is easy to remember. A moving one has to be watched for.
